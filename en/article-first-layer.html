First-Layer Problems, Warping, and Failed PLA Prints

Updated November 26, 2026

The first layer anchors an FDM print to the build surface. If it does not adhere consistently, the part may shift, warp, or fail later in the job. The most reliable approach is to inspect the pattern, check the basics, and change one thing at a time.

Use the printer and build-plate manufacturer's instructions for calibration and cleaning. Settings vary between machines and materials.

Read the pattern before changing settings

Notice where the problem appears. If it happens across the whole bed, suspect a general setup issue. If it appears only in one area, inspect that region of the plate and the printer's leveling or mesh calibration. If only sharp corners lift, the model's geometry and cooling may be contributing.

Gaps between first-layer lines

Lines that do not touch can indicate that the nozzle is too far from the surface, the bed is uneven, or extrusion is inconsistent. Check the printer's first-layer calibration and verify that the plate is seated correctly. Avoid compensating for a mechanical problem by making large slicer changes.

Scraped or overly flattened lines

If the nozzle drags through the material or the layer looks excessively compressed, the nozzle may be too close, the surface may be uneven, or flow may be too high. Stop the test if the nozzle is contacting the plate. Recheck calibration using the printer's documented procedure.

Print detaches from the plate

Start with surface condition. Dust, fingerprints, and residue can make adhesion inconsistent. Clean the plate using the method recommended for its coating and allow it to dry. Also check that the first layer is consistent and that the build plate is correctly installed.

If the print still detaches, consider drafts, the model's contact area, and whether the selected material is appropriate for the surface and conditions.

One corner lifts while the rest stays flat

Localized lifting can be related to sharp corners, uneven cooling, drafts, or a small contact area. Compare the affected corner with the model orientation and the corresponding bed location. A brim or a geometry change may help some designs, but it should be selected for the specific model rather than applied automatically.

Warping begins later in the print

A first layer can look good while upper layers still warp as the part cools and contracts. Large flat areas and sharp corners are especially sensitive. Review room drafts, cooling, material guidance, and the model's shape. A suitable enclosure may be required for some materials; do not assume PLA, ABS, and other filaments behave the same way.

Check the build surface and calibration

  • Confirm the build plate is seated and secured as designed.
  • Clean the surface using the plate manufacturer's recommended method.
  • Run the printer's leveling or mesh procedure when appropriate.
  • Check nozzle height with the printer's documented calibration method.
  • Use a small first-layer test before starting a long print.

Change one variable at a time

Changing bed temperature, nozzle temperature, speed, flow, and first-layer height all at once makes troubleshooting unreliable. Identify the symptom, choose the most likely cause, make one small adjustment within the manufacturer's guidance, and test again.

When to revise the model

If the same corners or thin contact areas repeatedly fail, the geometry may be contributing to the problem. Rounded corners, a wider base, or a design split may help in some cases. Any design change should preserve the part's intended dimensions and use; do not change a customer's approved model without permission.

Quick troubleshooting checklist

SymptomFirst checks
Lines do not joinNozzle height, leveling, extrusion consistency
Nozzle scrapes the layerNozzle height and plate calibration
Whole print detachesSurface cleanliness, first layer, plate installation
One corner liftsDrafts, corner geometry, local surface condition
Upper layers warpCooling, room conditions, material guidance, model shape

Quick takeaway

Do not treat every first-layer failure as the same problem. Read where and how the defect appears, check surface condition and calibration, then test one change at a time. A short test is cheaper than discovering a preventable problem hours into a print.
